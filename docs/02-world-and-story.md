# 02 World & Story

## Bellwether

Bellwether sits inside the remains of an enormous circular structure built around a shaft descending into the earth. Houses cling to its stone rim. Bridges cross the opening. Freight platforms hang from chains. Warm light spills from workshops into the mist rising from below.

The structure predates the town by thousands of years. Nobody knows who built it.

Bellwether began as a mining camp. Then came an inn, a forge, a market, families, gardens, a school. Eventually people stopped saying they were staying temporarily. Now it's a proper town with an extremely improper foundation.

Most residents aren't adventurers. They're haulers, cooks, fishers, surveyors, smiths, herbalists, lift operators, and people who repair other people's terrible decisions. The dungeon supports all of them: ore feeds the forge, cave fish feed the kitchens, underground plants become medicine and lamp oil, and old machinery holds components nobody knows how to make anymore.

The town isn't waiting for someone to defeat the dungeon. The dungeon is its livelihood. That matters once you start finding out what might actually be down there.

## Floor Zero, and the other numbers

The townspeople call the settlement Floor Zero because that's what the ancient marker beside the main lift says.

**Change:** in the pitch, that's the only floor number anyone has ever found. Here it's the only one anyone has been able to *read*. The Floor Zero marker is carved stone. Every other marker in the dungeon is a dead panel that only shows its number when its station has power.

When you restore the Pump Station at the end of the first chapter, every panel in the Old Works lights up at once. The places you've been calling "the Galleries" and "the Pool" turn out to be Floor 2 and Floor 3. A region you thought you knew gets recontextualized in one moment, and from then on depth has a number.

That number becomes DanMachi-style bragging rights. The Survey Office posts the deepest floor anyone has reached, and people in town know yours.

## What the dungeon was

The dungeon was once a vast underground infrastructure complex. Not a fortress, not a mine. It held water systems, farming chambers, material processing plants, homes, transport networks, research facilities, and something much deeper that needed all of the above.

The builders disappeared, but parts of the complex kept running. Over centuries nature moved into the machinery. Roots broke into channels. Fish adapted to reservoirs. Creatures nested in warm engine rooms. Automatic systems kept doing jobs whose purpose no longer existed.

That's why the dungeon has both architecture and ecology. A chamber has enemies in it for a reason: it's warm, it has fresh water, something there produces spores, a defence system still treats everything as an intruder, predators gather where migrating creatures pass, or a damaged machine is changing the environment.

## What lives down there

| Kind | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Wildlife** | Animals adapted to the dark, the water, and the warmth | Cave fish, Scuttlers, Slagbacks |
| **Constructs** | Maintenance machines still following old orders | Rivet Sentries, Lampwardens, the Sluicekeeper |
| **Overgrowth** | Fungus and root organisms grown into the machinery | Bellcaps, Sinkmaw tunnels |
| **The Displaced** | Creatures from far below, pushed upward by changes in the deep | The Irregular |

The Displaced are rare in the upper floors, and every one of them is a clue.

## Cores

**Change:** new, from DanMachi's magic stones.

Anything that lives long enough down there grows a core, a crystallized knot of the dungeon's energy. Constructs run on them. Bellwether burns them in its lamps and furnaces. The town's warm windows are, quite literally, lit by what delvers bring up.

Cores give combat a baseline income alongside gathering. The Survey Office buys them at fixed prices by grade. Later, resonance tools can burn them as charge, so selling a core and spending one become a real choice.

## Where things stand when you arrive

Bellwether has lived off the upper regions for years, and it's stagnating. The easy seams are thinning. The old machinery is failing. The main freight lift can't descend past its first station.

You arrive just after a tremor exposes a sealed passage beneath the old quarry. Inside is a working piece of ancient machinery, and evidence that the lift network can be restored.

That gives the campaign its shape: explore each region, understand its infrastructure, recover what's needed to restore the next connection, and bring the discoveries back into town life. Bosses guard important places, but progress is rarely just "kill the boss, the door opens." You might drain a flooded tunnel, restore power to a station, repair a counterweight, evict a creature nesting inside a mechanism, find a missing operating sequence, or build protective gear for a hostile environment.

## The Root Engine

Ancient records mention a place called the Root Engine. Nobody agrees on what it is:

- **Mara:** a power source. Get it running and every lift works again.
- **Oren:** a forge, where the builders made things he can only dream of.
- **Iona:** a city. The records describe people, not just machines.
- **Tavi:** treasure, obviously.
- **Old Bellwether families:** the thing holding the dungeon up. Leave it alone.

## The mystery

As you descend you realize the upper dungeon isn't dying. Something below is redirecting its resources. Water levels are changing. Old machines are waking. Creatures are moving upward. The Irregular shouldn't exist on Floor 2.

The question isn't just "what's at the bottom?" It's **what is the dungeon preparing to do?**

The Quiet City makes it personal. The builders weren't just workers serving a machine. They lived here, and something convinced all of them to leave, or to go further down.

## The answer *(proposal, not decided)*

The story needs a spine before we write much dialogue. Two candidates:

**A. The owners are coming home (recommended).** The builders went below to wait something out: a catastrophe on the surface, or a long sleep. The Root Engine sustains them. For centuries it idled. Now it's waking, pulling water and power downward, restarting production lines, driving wildlife up. The dungeon is getting ready for its owners to return, and Bellwether has been living in the entrance hall of someone else's house. The final decision is what the town does about that: share, negotiate, hand it over, or shut the Engine down.

**B. The bloom.** The Root Engine is a growth engine its builders deliberately throttled. It has started to bloom. Left alone it will grow upward and swallow Bellwether, or transform it into something extraordinary. The final decision is whether to throttle it again or guide the bloom.

A fits the "home" theme best, explains the Quiet City, needs no villain, and matches the Sluicekeeper being a machine doing its old job rather than an evil one.

## Tone

Warm, grounded, curious. Humour comes from the townsfolk. Wonder and unease come from below. It's not grimdark: no piles of bodies in corridors, and danger is natural or mechanical rather than malicious.

The story should have a real conclusion. Reaching the Root Engine resolves what's happening and gives Bellwether a future. It doesn't delete the dungeon or make gathering pointless.
